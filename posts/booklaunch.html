Book Launch Event: Islamic and Islamicate Architecture in the Americas

February 20, 2026

Yesterday, I attended the book launch event for Professor Olivia Wolf’s edited volume at Loyola University. She invited me to be the moderator, and I was happy to join colleagues who contributed their excellent research to the volume.

The volume, Islamic and Islamicate Architecture in the Americas: Transregional Dialogues and Manifestations, was published by Intellect Books as the fourteenth and latest volume in the Critical Studies in Architecture of the Middle East series. It brings new perspectives and recent research on both historic and modern architecture, landscape, and urban design in the Middle East and in areas influenced by global diasporic communities. The publication of this latest volume in the series, Islamic and Islamicate Architecture in the Americas, aligns with a time in which the study of Islamic art, architecture, material culture, and archaeology is becoming increasingly globalized and expanding in chronological scope. As art historians of Islamic art and architecture, this moment presents us with an opportunity to identify and address longstanding gaps in scholarship within the discipline.

As the moderator, I’d like to share some thoughts on how the chapters connect and highlight the volume's main topics and methods. This book offers new insights from scholars of Islamic art and architecture, focusing on the often-overlooked tradition of Islamic architecture in the Americas. The chapters explore topics from sixteenth-century Mexico and New Granada to colonial Peru, as well as modern and contemporary sites created by Muslim diaspora communities in Brazil, Canada, Chile, and the United States. Recent scholarship has identified a significant gap in research on Islamic architecture in the West. More recently, art historian Professor Bernard O’Kane has described this omission and the field’s overlooked regions as an academic “elephant in the room,” urging research that transcends the traditional geographic boundaries of Islamic art and architecture, which have historically focused on the Mediterranean, Europe, North Africa, and Western and Central Asia. This volume responds to this call by examining the visual and historiographic legacies of the Islamic world as manifested in the built environments of the Americas.

The contributors challenge established nineteenth-century geographical boundaries and chronologies by demonstrating the migration and adaptation of Islamic architectural elements, building techniques, decorative programs, and cultural practices throughout the Americas and their related networks. This significant architectural and artistic legacy has been largely neglected in existing scholarship on Islamic architecture, urbanism, and the built environment. Accordingly, this volume offers scholars and students new frameworks and case studies to deepen understanding of architectural designs, adaptations, and shared visual vocabularies by situating the Americas within the broader historical context of global Islamic art, architecture, culture, and history.

By fostering a pluralistic dialogue, chapters and case studies move beyond descriptive analysis, conventional chronologies and geographical boundaries of the field of Islamic architecture to examine how the mobility and migration of architectural forms and narratives acquire new significance in diverse environments, thereby initiating new debates from previously neglected perspectives.
