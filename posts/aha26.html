Circulations in Premodern Maritime and Silk Road Inter-Asia

January 12, 2026

Since 1884, the American Historical Association's annual meeting has served as a central forum for historians to discuss ideas, present new research, and reconsider historical narratives. As the largest gathering of historians in the United States, it brings together scholars across fields. The meeting’s panels, workshops, informal discussions, and book exhibits create an environment for three days of sustained academic engagement.

Our panel, Circulations in Premodern Maritime and Silk Road Inter-Asia: Perspectives from the Field Museum, demonstrated this commitment to intellectual exchange by bringing together specialists in China, Southeast Asia, Mongol Eurasia, and the medieval Islamicate world. The discussion focused on the premodern history of Inter-Asian trade, emphasizing trade, movement, and material culture across the Indian Ocean.

As scholars specializing in economic and material history, cultural and religious exchange, anthropology, art history, and archaeology, we gathered at the Field Museum of Natural History in Chicago to examine shipwreck artifacts from the Java Sea. The panel focused on material culture, particularly ceramics and bronze mirrors. Presentations employed the concept of materiality to investigate connections among ports, trade routes, and societies across premodern Inter-Asia, tracing the movement of trade objects and material practices by land and sea. Panelists explored topics including maritime activity in Southeast Asian port cities of the Gulf of Siam and Melaka Straits, shipwreck evidence for the transmission and transformation of bronze mirror production between China and the medieval Islamic world, inscriptions on shipwreck ceramics, and the use of diverse numeric systems by merchants along Indian Ocean maritime trade routes. Following the presentations, our commentator synthesized the connections among the papers and facilitated a discussion of each panelist’s approach to material and methodology with both the audience and panelists. The discussion addressed patterns of Inter-Asian trade and consumption from the ninth to the early fifteenth centuries.

With the support of Field Museum specialists, artifacts from the twelfth- and thirteenth-century Java Sea Shipwreck in the museum’s collection were displayed during our session. Attendees had the opportunity to examine these artifacts from premodern trade routes directly. Because these objects are typically inaccessible in museum storage, the session attracted significant interest from historians of Asia, Islam, maritime trade, art, and museums.

It was a true pleasure to join this panel alongside friends and colleagues. Sharing my ongoing research and discovering new insights into the Java Sea shipwrecks and Indian Ocean material culture made the experience especially rewarding.
